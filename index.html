I am an experienced, effective professional treating a broad range of psychiatric issues and conditions. I primarily do Individual Counseling, Couples Counseling, and Group Counseling. I specialize in working with adult men, couples, and young adult males, but I also work well with women. When you begin counseling with me, you can expect a thoughtful, collaborative process focused on insight, self-understanding, and meaningful change.

Our work is grounded in the belief that lasting growth comes from understanding why you think, feel, and respond the way you do and not just managing symptoms. We will explore your life story, including formative relationships and experiences that have shaped your identity, patterns, and emotional responses. My approach is insight-oriented and informed by psychodynamic principles, with a strong emphasis on person-centered work. I also use gentle Socratic questioning and CBT-based strategies to help you identify and reframe unhelpful thought patterns when they are getting in your way.

Sessions are guided by what is most important to you. Each week, we’ll check-in on how you’re really doing; not just what happened, but how you’re feeling, coping, and applying insights from our work together. Rather than following a rigid agenda, we focus on the themes that naturally emerge, trusting that the most important issues will surface when given space. You won’t be overloaded with generic or busy-work homework. When between-session practices are suggested, they are purposeful, practical, and tailored to your goals. Much of the work happens through reflection, increased awareness, and learning to respond to yourself and others with greater clarity and intention.

At the core of my work is the belief that you already possess the wisdom needed to move forward. At times, that wisdom can be obscured by ego defenses, old survival strategies, or cognitive distortions. My role is to help you recognize and clear those obstacles so you can live more authentically and engage in healthier, more satisfying relationships.

Counseling with me is not about quick fixes. It is a process of understanding yourself more fully and creating sustainable change, one session at a time.

Individual Counseling

Individual counseling begins with a thorough intake, clinical formulation, and individualized treatment plan. Sessions are typically held weekly and are tailored to the client’s goals, strengths, and challenges. Treatment is Person-Centered in nature and integrates evidence-based approaches such as Psychodynamic Therapy, CBT, MI, DBT, and REBT.

The goal is to provide a safe, supportive, and structured space where clients can gain insight, build skills, and create lasting change in their lives.

Couples Counseling

Couples counseling begins with a comprehensive relationship assessment and collaborative treatment plan designed to identify patterns of conflict, communication breakdowns, and emotional disconnection. Evidence-based tools such as Prepare/Enrich are used to assess relationship strengths, growth areas, and compatibility factors. Treatment is informed by Gottman Method principles, with a focus on improving communication, managing conflict, strengthening emotional connection, and increasing mutual understanding. Therapy is also increasingly guided by Imago Relationship Therapy, which helps couples understand how early relational experiences influence adult partnership dynamics. Couples are supported in developing healthier interaction patterns, repairing trust, and building a more secure and intentional relationship.

INTRODUCING

What Is Man-To-Man Counseling?


Man-To-Man Counseling is a therapy practice focused primarily on helping men navigate the real-life challenges that can leave them feeling stressed-out, stuck, overwhelmed, disconnected, or unsure of what to do next.
I work with men dealing with anxiety, depression, anger, relationship problems, stress, life transitions, low self-esteem, perfectionism, emotional disconnection, and questions about identity, purpose, and direction.
My approach is practical, straightforward, and collaborative. I combine Person-Centered Therapy, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, and principles from Solution-Focused Brief Therapy to help clients better understand themselves, identify the patterns that are getting in the way, and make meaningful changes.
The goal is not simply to talk about problems. It is to understand what is happening, determine what needs to change, and build a realistic path forward.
Man-To-Man Counseling is designed to be a place where men can talk honestly, without judgment or pressure to be someone they are not.
The focus is simple: better self-understanding, stronger relationships, clearer direction, and real-life change.

Common Questions From Men

1. How Can Counseling Help Me Become a Better Husband, Partner, or Father?


Many men come into counseling because something is not working in their relationships.
Maybe your wife or partner says you are emotionally unavailable. Maybe you are arguing more, growing distant, or losing patience with your kids. Or maybe nothing is terribly wrong, but you know you are not as connected to your family as you want to be.
You may think:
“I’m working hard. I’m providing for my family. I’m doing everything I’m supposed to do. Why isn’t that enough?”
Providing matters. Being dependable matters. Working hard matters.
Those are important ways many men show love.
But the people closest to you need something else too.
They need to know you.
Sometimes men become so focused on taking care of their families that they slowly become disconnected from them.
You go to work. Pay the bills. Handle problems. Take care of the house. Get everyone where they need to go.
You are doing a lot.
But eventually the relationship can start to feel more like managing a household than sharing a life.
Counseling gives you a chance to step back and look at how you show up in your relationships.
What happens when your partner is upset with you?
Do you become defensive? Get angry? Shut down? Try to fix the problem when she really wants you to listen?
Do you avoid difficult conversations?
When you are stressed, who gets the best version of you? Your family, or has your patience already been used up everywhere else?
These questions are not about guilt. They are about awareness.
Once you recognize your patterns, you can begin changing the ones that are not working.
You can learn to listen without immediately defending yourself. Stay engaged instead of shutting down. Recognize frustration before you lose your temper. Communicate what you actually need. Repair the relationship after an argument.
Being a better husband, partner, or father does not mean becoming perfect.
You are still going to have bad days and say the wrong thing sometimes.
What matters is what you do next.
Can you take responsibility? Can you reconnect after conflict? Can you tell the people you love what they mean to you? Can you let them know what is happening inside you instead of expecting them to guess?
The people you love do not need you to be perfect.
They need you to be present.
Counseling can help you become more self-aware, more intentional, and better equipped to handle the difficult moments every relationship experiences.
Because being a good husband, partner, or father is not only about what you provide.
It is also about the relationship you build.

2. Why Do I Get So Angry or Irritated Over Small Things?


Maybe you lose patience with your kids, snap at your wife or partner, get unusually frustrated in traffic, or find yourself irritated with people around you.
Sometimes you know you overreacted. You can see it afterward. But in the moment, the reaction feels completely justified.
Then you calm down and wonder, “Why did I get so worked up about that?”
One thing I have learned from working with men is that anger is often the emotion we notice, but it is not always where the problem started.
There may be something underneath it.
Stress. Anxiety. Hurt. Exhaustion. Fear. Rejection. Feeling criticized, disrespected, overwhelmed, or unappreciated.
A lot of men were never taught how to recognize those emotions. We learned how to solve problems, push through difficulty, and handle responsibility.
Those are useful skills, but they do not always work well with emotions.
When you do not understand what you are feeling, everything can start coming out as frustration.
I often think of anger as a warning light on a dashboard. The light tells you something needs attention, but it does not necessarily tell you exactly what is wrong.
The goal is not simply to turn off the warning light. It is to understand why it came on.
That matters because the people closest to you may only experience the anger. They may not see the stress, hurt, fear, or pressure underneath it.
Over time, your partner may become cautious about what she says. Your kids may learn to stay away when Dad is irritated. You may find yourself regretting things you said or did.
So instead of only asking, “How do I control my anger?” I encourage men to ask:
“What is my anger trying to tell me?”
In counseling, we slow things down. What consistently sets you off? What thoughts run through your mind? What are you feeling besides anger? What is actually bothering you?
Once you understand that, you have more choices.
The goal is not to eliminate anger. Anger is normal and sometimes appropriate.
The goal is to understand it well enough that you stay in control of what happens next.

3. Why Do I Shut Down Emotionally or Pull Away in My Relationship?


Your wife or partner may say things like, “I don't feel emotionally connected to you,”, “I don’t know what you’re feeling,” or “I feel like I can’t reach you.”
From your perspective, you may not be trying to pull away.
Maybe you do not know what to say. Maybe every conversation seems to become an argument. Maybe you have learned that whatever you say somehow makes things worse.
So eventually, you stop saying anything.
You get quiet. Change the subject. Look at your phone. Go to another room. Tell yourself you just need some space.
You may experience that as trying to keep things from getting worse.
Your partner may experience it as rejection.
That is how two people who care about each other can get caught in a painful cycle.
One person tries harder to talk about the relationship. The other becomes overwhelmed and pulls away. The more one pursues, the more the other withdraws.
For many men, shutting down is not about not caring. Sometimes it is exactly the opposite.
You may care so much about the relationship that conflict feels overwhelming. You do not want to say the wrong thing, disappoint your partner, feel criticized, or make the argument worse.
Withdrawal feels safer.
The problem is that what feels safe to you may feel very unsafe to your partner.
One of the most important things I help men understand is that you do not have to choose between arguing and shutting down.
There is another option.
You can learn to stay engaged in difficult conversations without feeling like you have to solve everything, defend yourself, or come up with the perfect answer.
That starts with understanding what is happening inside you.
Are you hurt? Anxious? Embarrassed? Feeling criticized or inadequate? Afraid of losing the relationship?
If you do not understand what you are feeling, it is very difficult to explain it to someone else.
Counseling can help you recognize what is happening earlier and communicate it more clearly.
Sometimes progress sounds as simple as:
“I’m getting overwhelmed right now, but I don’t want to avoid this. Give me 20 minutes to clear my head and then let’s come back to it.”
That is very different from walking away and never returning to the conversation.
You do not have to become more emotional or change your personality.
You just need to learn how to stay connected when conversations become difficult.
Sometimes emotional connection does not require having all the right words.
Sometimes it simply means staying in the room.

4. Why Is It So Hard for Men to Talk About Their Feelings, and Does It Actually Help?


A lot of men come into my office with very little interest in talking about their feelings.
I get it.
If you have a problem, you probably want to solve it. If something is broken, you want to fix it. So being told to “get in touch with your feelings” may sound vague or pointless.
Here is how I think about it:
Your emotions are information.
They tell you something about what matters to you, what you need, what you are afraid of, and what may not be working in your life.
Anger may tell you that something feels unfair or a boundary has been crossed.
Anxiety may tell you that you are afraid of what might happen.
Resentment may tell you that you have been saying yes when you wanted to say no.
Sadness may tell you that you have lost something important.
Feeling numb may tell you that you have become disconnected from something that matters.
The emotion is not always the problem. Sometimes it is information about the problem.
And when you ignore that information, it usually does not disappear. It may come out as irritability, withdrawal, overworking, drinking, relationship problems, or simply a vague sense that you are unhappy.
You cannot fix a problem you do not understand.
Sometimes men spend years trying to solve the wrong problem.
You may think you need a new job when you are really missing purpose.
You may think you are angry when you are actually hurt.
You may think you are lazy when you are exhausted.
You may think your marriage is the problem when you have become disconnected from yourself.
Understanding your emotions helps you understand yourself.
That is the real point of talking about feelings.
The goal is not to analyze every emotion or turn you into someone you are not. It is to notice patterns.
What frustrates you? What makes you anxious? What causes you to shut down? What do you resent? What makes you feel alive? What are you avoiding?
Those questions provide useful information.
Understanding your emotions is not about becoming more emotional.
It is about becoming more self-aware.
And self-awareness gives you choices.
You do not need to become a different kind of man.
You just need to understand the man you already are.

5. Why Do I Feel Stuck in Life Even Though I Have a Good Job, Family, or Successful Career?


This is one of the most common questions I hear from men.
From the outside, your life may look pretty good. You have a career. You provide for your family. You’ve checked many of the boxes you were told would lead to a satisfying life.
But something still feels off.
You may feel restless, disconnected, unmotivated, or like you’re simply going through the motions. Sometimes that leads to another question:
“What’s wrong with me? I should be grateful.”
The truth is, nothing may be wrong with you at all.
Many men spend years focused on responsibility. They work hard, take care of others, solve problems, and keep moving forward. Somewhere along the way, they stop asking themselves an important question:
“Am I actually living the life I want, or am I simply fulfilling my obligations?”
Those are not the same thing.
Feeling stuck often develops slowly. You become so busy meeting expectations that you lose touch with your own goals, interests, and identity. Life begins to feel more like a list of responsibilities than something you genuinely enjoy.
For some men, that shows up as burnout or frustration. Others withdraw, become irritable, lose motivation, or simply feel numb.
Often, feeling stuck is less about your circumstances and more about feeling disconnected from who you are and what matters most to you.
Counseling can help you figure out what is keeping you stuck. It may be fear of change, perfectionism, anxiety, unresolved pain, people-pleasing, burnout, or years of putting everyone else’s needs ahead of your own.
My goal is not to tell you how to live your life. It is to help you gain clarity, reconnect with what matters to you, and develop practical steps toward moving forward.
I believe counseling should lead to real change, not just interesting conversations.
You do not have to blow up your life to improve it. Sometimes meaningful change starts with a few intentional decisions that move you closer to the man you want to become.

Group Counseling

Group counseling provides a structured, therapist-led environment where individuals can develop insight, skills, and accountability alongside others facing similar challenges. Groups are intentionally designed to balance psychoeducation, interpersonal process, and practical skill-building. Participants benefit not only from professional guidance, but also from shared experience, feedback, and normalization that occurs within the group setting. Group counseling is particularly effective for addressing issues such as emotional regulation, relationships, stress, anger, and personal growth. The group format fosters connection, perspective-taking, and real-world application of therapeutic skills, helping participants move from insight to meaningful, sustainable change.
